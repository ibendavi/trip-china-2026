China 2026 — Route 3 Day-by-Day

The Grand Tour: Guizhou → Gannan → Silk Road

Itzhak & Romy · Aug 29 – Sep 14, 2026 · draft for discussion

The shape of it

Three acts, two internal flights, one long rail leg. Act I is subtropical village China — Dong and Miao, indigo, sour fish soup, karst. Act II is the Tibetan plateau at its least-visited: Labrang, Langmusi, and the rock towers of Zhagana. Act III is high desert — the rainbow Danxia, the Silk Road's western passes, and the Mogao caves.

Act I  Days 1–7 · Guizhou · fly in to Guiyang, loop east then west

Act II  Days 8–13 · Gannan · fly Guiyang→Lanzhou (1h35m), then a driving loop

Act III  Days 14–17 · Silk Road · rail to Zhangye, on to Dunhuang, fly out

You are back in Beijing the evening of Sep 14 — a full night's sleep before Sep 15, rather than landing on the morning you need to be working.

Two honest warnings

Day 4 and Day 13 are the hard transfer days. Rongjiang→Leishan (~4h of mountain road) and Zhagana→Lanzhou (~7h) are both long. They are the price of reaching places without airports. Everything else is a comfortable half-day or less.

This is a full plan with little padding. September rain closes mountain roads in both Guizhou and Gannan occasionally. Treat Day 7 (Qingyan) and Day 16 (western desert) as the two flexible days you can sacrifice if weather forces it.

Act I · Guizhou — villages, indigo, and karst

Days 1–7 · Private guide-interpreter and driver throughout. Nights are a mix of village guesthouses and simple county-town hotels — this is the low-amenity stretch of the trip, and the one where a guide matters most.

Day 1Sat Aug 29Into the Dong country

Morning flight from Beijing to Guiyang, then high-speed rail south to Congjiang and a short drive into Zhaoxing. Zhaoxing is the largest Dong village in China — five drum towers, one for each clan, and wind-and-rain bridges over the stream running through the middle. It has become genuinely touristy, so treat it as a comfortable base rather than the destination; the quiet is up the hill tomorrow.

MoveBeijing→Guiyang ~3h · rail to Congjiang ~1h · drive ~1hNightZhaoxing

See itZhaoxing Dong Village·Photo-heavy guide

Day 2Sun Aug 30Tang'an terraces on foot

Hike the old stone path from Zhaoxing up to Tang'an, a small terraced Dong hamlet on the ridge above — roughly two hours up through rice terraces, with the valley opening behind you the whole way. Tang'an is an open-air ecomuseum and much quieter than Zhaoxing. Walk back down in the afternoon, or continue along the ridge if the weather is good. Evening: Dong polyphonic choir singing drifts through the village after dark, unamplified and unstaged if you find the right courtyard.

Day 3Mon Aug 31Basha, and west to Rongjiang

Morning at Basha — the Miao village whose men still carry matchlock rifles, the only civilian community in China licensed to do so, and who wear hand-woven hemp dyed with tree bark. The gunfire-and-lusheng welcome is arranged per group rather than run to a public timetable, so have the guide fix it in advance, and go early before the coaches. Afternoon: on to Rongjiang and the small Dong village of Zhanli, cut off enough that it still has its own customs.

Day 4Tue Sep 1Dali indigo, then the long road north

Morning in Dali Dong village on the Duliu River — six hundred years old, wooden balconies over the stream, and the indigo tradition that defines the place: women harvest the leaves from the hillsides, ferment them into paste, and dye cloth that takes about a year to finish a single bolt. Then the trip's first hard drive, north into the Leigong mountains, arriving at Langde in the afternoon for the long-skirt Miao village and its silver work.

Day 5Wed Sep 2Leigongshan ridges

A full day walking the ridges that connect the Miao hamlets above Langde — the kind of hiking where you pass through three villages and see no other outsiders. Lunch is sour fish soup, ideally cooked in a hot stone pot: fermented rice water and tomato, sharp and clean, and the dish the whole province is built around. Afternoon with a silversmith or blacksmith if the guide can arrange it — Leishan is the centre of Miao silver work.

Day 6Thu Sep 3Getu River karst

Drive west out of the Miao mountains to the Getu River, where the karst stops being scenery and becomes architecture: the Great Arch is one of the largest natural rock spans anywhere, with a cave system beneath it and a river running through. Local climbers free-climb the arch face. Boat in, walk the caves, and see the karst without the Yangshuo crowds.

Day 7Fri Sep 4Qingyan, and the food night

Back toward Guiyang, stopping at Qingyan — a Ming-dynasty walled garrison town thirty kilometres south of the city, and the right place to end Act I because it is a genuine eating destination. The list: Qingyan pig's trotters simmered with ginger, rice tofu, tofu balls, siwawa (vegetables rolled in a thin pancake), and a bowl of changwang noodles. Stay into the evening — the coach crowds leave at dusk and the lanes are worth walking after dark.

Act II · Gannan — the Tibetan plateau, without the permits

Days 8–13 · A second operator takes over here. This is Amdo Tibetan country in southern Gansu — no permits needed, unlike the Tibet Autonomous Region, and very few foreign visitors. Altitude runs roughly 2,900–3,000 m, high enough to notice on the first day but not high enough to need an acclimatization plan.

Day 8Sat Sep 5South-west to south of the Yellow River

Fly Guiyang to Lanzhou in the morning — the hinge of the whole trip, and mercifully short. From Lanzhou, drive south-west into the grasslands to Xiahe. The landscape changes completely within two hours: terraced green gives way to open high pasture, and the prayer flags start. Arrive in time for the evening kora, when the pilgrims walk.

MoveGuiyang→Lanzhou 1h35m · drive to Xiahe ~3h (220 km)NightXiahe

See itXiahe & Labrang·Gannan overview

Day 9Sun Sep 6Labrang

Labrang is one of the six great Gelug monasteries and, outside Tibet proper, the most important Tibetan Buddhist institution in China — founded 1709, still a working university with hundreds of monks. Its kora is 3.5 km, the longest pilgrim circuit in Tibetan Buddhism, lined with prayer wheels; walking it at dawn alongside the pilgrims is the thing to do here. Afternoon out to the Sangke grasslands, or the Ganjia basin with its octagonal walled ruin, and a visit to a Tibetan family.

MoveLocal, short drivesNightXiahe

See itLabrang Monastery·Xiahe & Sangke grassland

Day 10Mon Sep 7Langmusi, on the provincial line

Drive south to Langmusi, a small monastery town straddling the Gansu–Sichuan border — the provincial line runs down the middle, with Sertri Gompa on the Gansu side and the 600-year-old Kirti Gompa on the Sichuan side. Alpine meadows and forested ridges surround it; the comparison people reach for is rural Austria, which is not quite right but not wrong either. Gahai Lake and open grassland on the way.

Day 11Tue Sep 8Namo gorge, then into Zhagana

Morning hike up the Namo gorge behind Kirti Gompa — a narrow limestone cleft that opens into high meadow, a couple of hours up and back, and the sky-burial ground sits on the ridge above town (approach only if your guide says it is appropriate that day). Afternoon: the short drive to Zhagana, which is the reason this act exists.

MoveHike ~3h · Langmusi→Zhagana ~2hNightZhagana

See itZhagana trekking·Gannan itineraries

Day 12Wed Sep 9Zhagana, all day

Zhagana means "stone box" in Tibetan: a natural amphitheatre of vertical limestone towers with four Tibetan villages, barley terraces and wooden houses stacked on the valley floor inside it. Three main trails climb out of the basin for the panoramic view down over the villages; take the longest one you have daylight for. This is the solitude core of the trip — almost no foreign visitors reach it, and the walking is as good as anything in the Alps with none of the traffic.

Day 13Thu Sep 10The long road out

The trip's longest drive, back north through the grasslands to Lanzhou. Break it at Hezuo or Linxia; the Yellow River appears again on the approach. An early night in the city, because tomorrow is a rail day. If you have energy left, the Gansu Provincial Museum is the best Silk Road collection in the country and a good primer for Act III — free but reservation-only, and September falls inside the window when the original Bronze Galloping Horse is on display rather than the replica.

MoveZhagana→Lanzhou ~7hNightLanzhou

See itGansu Provincial Museum·The Flying Horse of Gansu

Act III · The Silk Road — desert, colour, and the caves

Days 14–17 · The infrastructure gets easy here: high-speed rail between cities, day drivers for the desert sites. September is the ideal window — dry, clear, and before the October Golden Week crush. Note you are skipping Jiayuguan's Great Wall fort deliberately, since Romy will see the Wall properly from Beijing after Sep 15.

Day 14Fri Sep 11Rainbow rock

High-speed rail north-west to Zhangye, into the Hexi Corridor — the thin strip of oasis between desert and mountain that the entire Silk Road had to thread. Late afternoon at the Danxia landforms: sandstone laid down in stripes of red, orange, cream and green, tilted on end and stripped bare. Go late, roughly two hours before sunset when the light is low and the colour saturates; the quieter Binggou canyon nearby is the version without the crowds.

MoveLanzhou→Zhangye by rail ~3hNightZhangye

See itZhangye Danxia geopark·Photographer's guide

Day 15Sat Sep 12Dawn colour, then deeper into the desert

Back to the Danxia at opening for the dawn light and an empty park — worth the alarm, and the colours are deepest just after rain if you get lucky. Then west to Dunhuang, the last real oasis before the desert proper. Arrive late afternoon and go straight to Mingsha Shan: dunes several hundred metres high with Crescent Lake, a spring-fed pool shaped like its name, sitting in a hollow between them and somehow never buried in all the centuries it has been recorded. Climb for sunset.

MoveZhangye→Dunhuang by rail ~5h or short flight — let the operator pickNightDunhuang

See itMingsha Shan & Crescent Lake·Echoing-Sand Mountain

Day 16Sun Sep 13The western passes

A full day out into the desert west of Dunhuang, and the emptiest landscape of the trip. Yumen Pass — the Jade Gate — is a lone rammed-earth block on gravel plain, the checkpoint everything bound for Central Asia once passed through. Nearby, eroded ramparts of the Han-dynasty Great Wall, older than the Ming brickwork tourists know, built of reeds and sand. Then the Yardang geopark, wind-carved rock formations in an old lakebed that the Chinese call the Ghost City; stay for sunset if the guide is willing.

MoveFull-day car ~8:30am–6:30pm, no public transport out thereNightDunhuang

See itYumen Pass·Yardang geopark

Day 17Mon Sep 14Mogao, and out

The Mogao Grottoes to finish: seven hundred caves cut into a cliff over a thousand years, painted floor to ceiling, the greatest surviving repository of Buddhist art anywhere. Take the 9am English tour — the ¥258 "A" ticket covers eight caves plus the digital films. Remember foreigners cannot book this through the WeChat system; the allocation is sold in person at the Digital Exhibition Centre, so have the operator handle it days ahead. Afternoon flight to Beijing, landing in the evening.

What to hand the operators

Split this at Day 7. Send Act I to the Guizhou operator and Acts II–III to the Gansu operator, and ask each to price their half against it. Points to make explicit:

  • Guizhou: we want Zhanli and Dali, not Xijiang Qianhu. Confirm the driver has personally driven Rongjiang→Leishan. Ask them to pre-arrange the Basha welcome and, if possible, a silversmith visit and one village meal.
  • Gannan: confirm lodging inside Zhagana rather than in Diebu town, and ask whether the Day 13 drive can break somewhere worth an hour rather than being pure transit.
  • Silk Road: Mogao tickets are the one genuine booking risk — get written confirmation of how and when they will secure the English tour. Also ask them to compare rail vs. flight for Zhangye→Dunhuang on that specific date.
  • All three: what happens if rain closes a road. You want to hear a specific alternative, not reassurance.
